Did you know the biggest robbery in American history, at the time, was pulled off by men in Halloween masks? In 1950, they walk right into the building of the company that guards Boston's money. They don't break a single lock. They don't fire a single shot. And in less than half an hour, they're gone. With almost 3 million dollars. For six years, the FBI can't touch them. Then, just five days before they'd be free forever, the whole plan falls apart. And it isn't a clue that brings them down. It's something the crew does to one of their own. And the money? Almost all of it is still missing.

This is the true story of the Brink's job.

Boston, 1948. In the North End, on Prince Street, stands a plain brick building. It belongs to Brink's, the armored truck company. Its whole job is keeping other people's money safe. Brink's is about to learn how hard that can be. Every evening, its trucks roll back in, full of money from banks and businesses across the city. The money goes upstairs, into a counting room, to be sorted and locked away. Tony Pino is a career thief with a big idea. He has done time before, and he isn't afraid to do it again. He knows this building. He knows those trucks. And he starts to wonder what it would take to walk right in and take that money. He can't do it alone. So, piece by piece, he builds a crew. By the end, there are 11 of them. There's his partner, Joe McGinnis. There's a driver, a lookout, and a team of men who will go inside. And there's a burglar named Joseph O'Keefe. Everyone calls him Specs. Remember that name.

Step one. Learn the building. The crew watches it for months. Pino wants to know this place better than the people who work there. They learn when the trucks come home. They learn which rooms are busy, and which are empty. This isn't a smash and grab. It's patient work. And late at night, when the building is quiet, they start slipping inside. They take nothing. They just look. Every visit is a risk. One wrong night, and the whole plan is over. But nobody ever catches them. They count the doors between the street and the money. There are five, and every one of them is locked. So that's the next problem.

Step two. The keys. One night, one of the crew stops at the first door. He doesn't pick the lock. He takes the whole lock out. He unscrews the brass cylinder, the part where the key goes, and carries it away. A key maker cuts a key to fit it. Then the lock goes back into the door, as if it was never gone. Nobody at Brink's notices a thing. So they do it again. And again. It's slow. It's risky. And it works. One lock at a time, over several months, they get a key for all five doors. Now they can walk from the street to the money without breaking anything. Think about that for a second. Without knowing it, the company that guards Boston's money has given its keys to the men who plan to rob it.

Step three. The getaway. In November 1949, the crew steals a truck from a Ford dealer. A green Ford, with an open back and wooden sides. That truck will matter later. Then they pick their outfits. Navy pea coats. Chauffeur caps. Gloves, and soft rubber shoes, so their footsteps make no sound. And rubber Halloween masks, so no one will ever see their faces. If anyone sees them, all they'll remember is a coat, a cap and a rubber face. Everything is ready.

January 17th, 1950. It's a winter evening, just after 7. The green truck pulls up near the building. The masked men climb out. A lookout keeps watch on the street. Key one, and the first door opens. Key two. Key three. Key four. Key five. No alarm goes off. They've walked this route before, in the dark. Upstairs, in the counting room, a few Brink's workers are putting the day's money away. They look up, and they see masks. And guns. The workers are tied up, hand and foot, and their mouths are taped shut. They can't move, and they can't call for help. Then the crew gets to work. They fill bag after bag. Cash. Checks. Money orders. Bonds. There's even more money in coins, but it's too heavy to carry, so they leave it behind. In a few minutes, the tables are bare. More than 1.2 million dollars in cash alone. They carry it all down the stairs and out to the truck. At 7:37, one of the workers gets free and raises the alarm. By then, the truck is gone. From the first door to the last bag, it's all over in less than half an hour.

By morning, it's front-page news across the country. People call it the crime of the century. The crew splits up the cash. Each man takes his share. And then they wait. Then they make a deal. Nobody spends big. Nobody talks. Not for six years. Why six? Because in Massachusetts, once six years have passed, they can never be charged for this robbery. Remember that number. Then they get rid of the truck. They cut it into pieces with a torch, smash the engine, and throw it away. In March, pieces of it turn up at a dump in Stoughton, south of Boston. The FBI now has a truck. What it doesn't have is a name.

The FBI throws everything at the case. Brink's offers a reward of 100,000 dollars. But the clues are thin. The crew left almost nothing behind. A chauffeur's cap. Some rope. Some tape. Agents talk to hundreds of people. They chase hundreds of dead ends. They have their suspects, Pino among them. But suspicion isn't proof. And the crew keeps its promise. Months go by. Then years. But the crew has a weak spot. Remember Specs O'Keefe, the burglar? In June 1950, just five months after the robbery, O'Keefe and another crew member are arrested in Pennsylvania. Not for the Brink's job. For a different burglary. O'Keefe gets three years in a Pennsylvania jail. He needs money for lawyers. He asks the others for his share. What comes back isn't enough. He feels cheated. In jail, there's a lot of time to think. And O'Keefe thinks about his money. Over the next few years, he keeps asking. And the others start to worry. A man who feels cheated is a man who might talk.

So they decide to solve the problem another way. They hire a hitman. He's a professional killer from New York. His name is Elmer Burke, and people call him Trigger. In 1954, Burke finds O'Keefe and opens fire with a machine gun. O'Keefe is hit in the chest and the wrist. Burke thinks he has done the job. He survives. Burke is arrested days later. That summer, gunmen break him out of a Boston jail in broad daylight. He's caught again a year later. Now O'Keefe knows exactly what his old friends think of him. And the FBI knows it too. Agents keep coming back to see him. They ask him the same question, again and again. And this time, he's listening.

It looks like the perfect crime. Six years of silence, and the clock is almost out. Remember that number? For the FBI, it's now a race against the calendar. The deadline is January 17th, 1956. On January 6th, 11 days before it runs out, Specs O'Keefe cracks. He tells the FBI everything. Who planned it, who went inside, and who drove the truck. The names. The doors. The keys. The truck. On January 12th, agents arrest six men. Pino and McGinnis are among them. It's just five days before the deadline. It was never a clue that brought them down. Not the cap. Not the rope. Not the truck. The crew tried to kill one of its own, and he turned them all in.

In October 1956, a Boston jury found eight of the men guilty. Every one of them got life in prison. One member of the crew died before the trial even started. O'Keefe, the man who talked, got just four years. He was out by 1960, and he even helped write a book about the robbery. Joe McGinnis died in prison. The Brink's job became one of the most famous cases in FBI history. Trigger Burke was executed in New York in 1958, for a different murder. Over the next 15 years, the rest were let out on parole, one by one. Specs O'Keefe lived until 1976.

Eleven men. Five keys. Less than half an hour, and six years minus five days. And the 1.2 million dollars in cash? Less than 60,000 dollars of it has ever turned up. The rest is still missing.
