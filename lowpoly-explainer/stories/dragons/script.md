## 01 · Cold open

In the year 793, England's chroniclers recorded something terrifying. "Immense sheets of light rushing through the air, and whirlwinds, and fiery dragons flying across the firmament."

Soon after, the Vikings came. They struck the holy island of Lindisfarne, and the chronicle remembered those dragons as a warning.

So what did medieval people actually believe a dragon was? The answer is stranger than any movie.

## 02 · What a dragon was

The word comes from the Latin "draco", a giant serpent.

Around the year 600, Isidore of Seville wrote the encyclopedia that medieval Europe trusted most after the Bible. His dragon was the largest creature on Earth. It had a crest and a small mouth, and its power was not in its teeth. It was in its tail.

The bestiaries, the illustrated animal books of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, added the details. A dragon waited on the paths elephants used, knotted its tail around their legs, and squeezed until they suffocated. It feared one animal above all: the panther, whose sweet breath drew every creature to it, except the dragon, which fled into hiding.

And in these books, the dragon did not breathe fire. It didn't need to.

## 03 · The hoard

The fire came from the poets.

In Beowulf, a thief steals a jewelled cup from a dragon sleeping on its treasure inside an ancient burial mound. The dragon wakes, and it burns the land, the king's own hall included. The old king goes out to face it. His sword shatters. His men run. Only young Wiglaf stays beside him. Together they kill the dragon, but Beowulf dies of his wounds.

It's often called medieval Europe's first fire-breathing dragon, and the poem survives in exactly one manuscript. In 1731 that manuscript was caught in a library fire. You can still see where its edges burned.

Further north, the Norse told of Fáfnir, so greedy for cursed gold that he killed his own father and became a serpent coiled on it. The hero Sigurd hid in a pit and stabbed upward as the dragon passed. When he roasted its heart and licked his burned finger, he could suddenly understand the birds.

## 04 · Saints against serpents

For the Church, the dragon was the Devil himself. The Book of Revelation describes a great red dragon with seven heads, thrown out of heaven by the archangel Michael.

Then there's Saint George. The real George was a Roman soldier who died around the year 303. His dragon didn't join the story for roughly seven hundred years.

The famous version comes from the Golden Legend, written in the 1260s. Outside a city called Silene, a dragon was fed sheep, then people, then children chosen by lottery, until the lot fell on the king's daughter. George wounded the beast with his lance, and the princess led it into the city on her belt, like a tame dog. Afterwards, it took four ox-carts to drag the body away.

The same book tells of a saint who escaped from inside a dragon's belly. Its own author called that story nonsense.

## 05 · Dragons of nations

Dragons flew over battlefields too. Roman cavalry rode behind dragon-shaped banners that hissed in the wind. On the Bayeux Tapestry, a dragon standard falls beside King Harold at Hastings, in 1066.

In a history written in Wales around 830, a king's tower keeps collapsing, because two dragons are fighting in a pool beneath it: a red one for the Britons, and a white one for the Saxons. A boy prophesies that the red will win. Henry Tudor carried that red dragon at Bosworth in 1485, though it only became the official flag of Wales in 1959.

In Poland, the oldest tale of the Wawel Dragon, from around 1200, has two princes kill it with a cattle hide stuffed with burning sulphur. Then one prince murders his brother, to take the credit.

## 06 · The evidence

People also thought they had proof. Around 1335, a huge skull was dug out of a gravel pit near Klagenfurt, in Austria, and put on display in the town hall as a dragon's skull. In 1840 it was identified. It belonged to a woolly rhinoceros. The city's stone dragon, carved in the 1590s, may even have been modelled on it.

In the Czech city of Brno, a dragon still hangs in the old town hall. It's a stuffed Nile crocodile. And in Kraków, giant bones hang on a chain outside the cathedral door.

## 07 · The edge of the map

Medieval maps did show dragons, sometimes coiled beneath the whole world. But the famous phrase "here be dragons" appears on exactly one known object: a copper globe about the size of a grapefruit, from the early 1500s. Off the coast of Asia, it reads, in Latin, "Hic sunt dracones". Here are dragons.

In 1855, an architect bought it in Paris for almost nothing, and let his children play with it.

## 08 · Ending

So a medieval dragon was many things. A light in the sky. A skull in the gravel. A crocodile from far away. The Devil himself.

Most of all, it was the shape people gave to everything they couldn't explain.
