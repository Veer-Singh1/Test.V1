# Medieval dragons: research notes

Research for a 4 to 5 minute documentary on dragons in the European Middle Ages (c. 500 to 1500) and how the belief carried into the 1500s. Compiled 16 Nov 2026.

**How this was sourced.** Wikipedia articles were read in full (`en.wikipedia.org`, fetched directly), and the claims below follow the citations those articles give. Other sites were blocked by the network policy. A fact or quotation that rests only on a web-search summary of a site that could not be opened is marked **(search only)**. Check those before they go on screen.

**Tags used on every bullet**
- **[MED]**: what a medieval (or older) source actually says
- **[LATER]**: later folklore, embellishment or modern invention
- **[DISPUTED]**: scholars disagree, or the evidence is thin
- **[MYTH]**: a popular claim that is wrong

**Quotations.** Translations marked PD are public domain: Ingram 1823, Caxton 1483 (edited by Ellis, 1900), Gummere 1910, King James Bible 1611, Bostock and Riley 1855, Bellows 1923. Anything else is a short modern translation, credited where it appears. Paraphrase it in narration, or clear it before quoting.

## 1. Lindisfarne, 793: "fiery dragons" in the sky

- **[MED]** Versions D and E of the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* record omens over Northumbria in 793: whirlwinds, lightning and "fyrenne dracan … on þam lifte fleogende" (fiery dragons flying in the air), then a famine, then the heathen raid on Lindisfarne. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne
- **Ingram's translation (1823, PD), entry for A.D. 793:** "This year came dreadful fore-warnings over the land of the Northumbrians, terrifying the people most woefully: these were immense sheets of light rushing through the air, and whirlwinds, and fiery dragons flying across the firmament. These tremendous tokens were soon followed by a great famine: and not long after, on the sixth day before the ides of January in the same year, the harrowing inroads of heathen men made lamentable havoc in the church of God in Holy-island, by rapine and slaughter." Wikipedia quotes it from the copy of Ingram's text in the Online Medieval and Classical Library. Ingram combined readings from several manuscripts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Age
- **The manuscripts.** D is the *Worcester Chronicle* (British Library, Cotton Tiberius B. iv), written in the mid-11th century, with northern material taken from 8th-century Northumbrian annals. E is the *Peterborough* or *Laud Chronicle* (Bodleian Library, Laud Misc. 636), copied by one scribe after a fire at Peterborough in 1116. The *Chronicle* itself was first compiled in Wessex around 893, under Alfred the Great, so neither copy is an eyewitness record. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Saxon_Chronicle
- **The date.** The manuscript gives "vi id Ianr" (8 January). Michael Swanton reads this as a slip for "vi id Iun", 8 June, the date given in the *Annals of Lindisfarne*. 8 June is the accepted date. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sack_of_Lindisfarne
- **[MED]** Alcuin wrote from Charlemagne's court: "Never before has such terror appeared in Britain as we have now suffered from a pagan race" (modern translation). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne
- **[DISPUTED]** No one knows what the chronicler saw. Aurora and meteors are modern guesses. Folklore did call meteors and will-o'-the-wisps "firedrakes" (fiery dragons). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firedrake_(folklore)

## 2. Beowulf's dragon

- **[MED] The story.** Fifty years into Beowulf's reign, a slave takes a jewelled cup from a sleeping dragon's hoard in a barrow. The dragon burns the Geats' homes, Beowulf's hall among them. Of his twelve men, only young Wiglaf stays to fight. Beowulf's sword shatters. Wiglaf stabs the dragon in the belly, Beowulf finishes it with his knife, and then he dies of his wounds. He is cremated and a barrow is raised for him, visible from the sea. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_dragon_(Beowulf) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beowulf
- **Gummere's translation (1910, PD), around line 2312:** "Then the baleful fiend its fire belched out, / and bright homes burned. The blaze stood high". **(search only:** https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/89/beowulf/1604/chapter-31/**)** The poem says the dragon guarded the hoard for "three hundred winters" (around line 2280) and gives its length as fifty feet (around line 3040). **(search only)**
- **[MED] What kind of dragon.** The poem calls it both *draca* (dragon) and *wyrm* (serpent), and gives it a venomous bite. It flies, but legs and wings are never mentioned. Wikipedia calls it the first fire-breathing dragon in literature, which holds for medieval literature (see section 11). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_dragon_(Beowulf)
- **One manuscript.** The poem (3,182 lines) survives only in the Nowell Codex, British Library Cotton Vitellius A. xv, copied c. 975 to 1025 by two scribes. The second takes over in mid-sentence at line 1939. The shelfmark points to the bookcase that carried a bust of the emperor Vitellius. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beowulf
- **The 1731 fire.** On 23 October 1731 fire swept Ashburnham House, Westminster, home of the Cotton library: 13 manuscripts were destroyed and about 200 badly damaged. Beowulf survived with charred margins. A contemporary records Richard Bentley leaping from a window with the *Codex Alexandrinus*. The poem was first transcribed in 1786 and first printed by Thorkelin in 1815. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_library · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowell_Codex
- **[DISPUTED] When it was written.** Proposed dates range from about 700 to 1000. Tolkien, Fulk and Neidorf argue for the 8th century, Craig Davis for the 890s, and Kevin Kiernan's school for the early 11th century. Only the manuscript's date is fixed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beowulf
- **[LATER]** Tolkien reused the stolen cup that wakes a dragon for Smaug in *The Hobbit* (1937). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_dragon_(Beowulf)

## 3. St George and the dragon

- **[MED] The man.** George was a Christian soldier martyred at Lydda (Diospolis) in Palestine. Tradition gives 23 April 303, under Diocletian. "No historical particulars of his life have survived" (Donald Attwater). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George
- **[LATER] The dragon came centuries later.** It was first attached to another soldier-saint, Theodore Tiro, in the 9th and 10th centuries. The oldest text in which George kills a dragon is Georgian, from the 11th century, and sets the story in Cappadocia. It reached the West through the Crusades in the 12th century. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George_and_the_Dragon
- **[MED] The *Golden Legend*.** Jacobus de Voragine compiled it around 1259 to 1266. More than 1,000 manuscripts survive. Among books printed before 1501 it had more editions than the Bible, and Caxton printed an English version in 1483. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend
- **[MED] The plot.** A venom-spewing dragon in a pond near "Silene" in Libya is fed two sheep a day, then a man and a sheep, then children chosen by lottery, until the lot falls on the king's daughter. George, a "tribune", makes the sign of the cross and wounds the dragon with his lance. The princess's girdle goes round its neck and it follows her like a "meek beast" (Caxton, PD; the Latin says "tamest dog"). George promises to kill it if the city is baptised: 15,000 men convert (20,000 in the Latin). He beheads the dragon, and four ox-carts haul the body away. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George_and_the_Dragon
- **[MED] How he became England's saint.** Bede lists him among the martyrs. Edward III put the Order of the Garter under his banner, probably in 1348. A church council in 1415 raised 23 April to a major feast. Edward the Confessor stayed England's traditional patron until 1552, when every other saint's banner was abolished. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George
- **[LATER]** The princess's name "Sabra", George's sword "Ascalon" and their marriage all come from Richard Johnson's *Seven Champions of Christendom* (1596). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_George_and_the_Dragon

## 4. Bestiaries and Isidore: what a dragon *was*

- **[MED] Isidore.** Isidore of Seville (c. 560 to 636) wrote the *Etymologiae* between about 600 and 625. Book XII covers animals. About 1,000 manuscripts survive, and it was the most influential book after the Bible in the Latin West for nearly a thousand years. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etymologiae
- **[MED] Isidore's dragon (*Etym.* XII.4.4–5), as the bestiaries repeat it:**
  - It is the largest of all serpents, indeed of all living things on earth.
  - It is often drawn out of caves into the air.
  - It is crested, with a small mouth and narrow pipes to breathe through.
  - Its strength is in its tail, not its teeth.
  - It lurks on elephant paths, knots its tail round their legs and suffocates them.
  - It is born in hot Ethiopia and India.

  **(search only:** University of Aberdeen edition, https://abdn.ac.uk/bestiary/ms24/f65v and https://abdn.ac.uk/bestiary/ms24/f66r ; https://bestiary.ca/beasts/beastsource262.htm**)** Also summarised in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_dragon
- **The Aberdeen translation (modern, not PD):** "Its strength lies not in its teeth but in its tail, and it kills with a blow rather than a bite." The bestiary reads the dragon as the Devil. **(search only)**
- **[MED] No venom.** Pliny, *Natural History* 29.20, in Bostock and Riley (1855, PD): "The dragon is a serpent destitute of venom." **(search only)**
- **[MED] The Aberdeen Bestiary.** Aberdeen University Library MS 24 is an English illuminated bestiary from around 1200, the sister manuscript of the Ashmole Bestiary. It was listed in 1542 in the Old Royal Library at Westminster and carries Henry VIII's shelf stamp. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen_Bestiary
- **[MED] The panther and other enemies.** In the *Physiologus* tradition (a Greek text from Alexandria, 2nd to 4th century), the panther wakes after three days' sleep and breathes out a sweet smell that draws every animal except the dragon, which hides in fear: good overcoming evil. The dragon also fears the shadow of the peridexion tree, and the ichneumon (mongoose) coats itself in mud to kill dragons. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panther_(legendary_creature) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bestiary · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peridexion_tree · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ichneumon_(medieval_zoology)
- **[MED] The modern image.** Bestiary MS Harley 3244 (around 1260) holds "the oldest recognizable image of a fully modern, Western dragon", with two pairs of wings and two pairs of legs. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_dragon
- **[DISPUTED]** Historians including Lindberg and Gravestock argue that bestiaries taught moral allegory rather than zoology. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bestiary

## 5. Norse dragons: Fáfnir, Níðhöggr and the dragon ships

- **[MED] Fáfnir.** Fáfnir kills his father for the dwarf Andvari's cursed gold, then lies on it on Gnitaheath and becomes a worm (*ormr*). In *Fáfnismál* (Codex Regius, about 1270) he is flightless and snake-like and blows out venom. *Völsunga saga* (late 13th century) gives him shoulders. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fáfnir · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Völsunga_saga
- **[MED] The kill.** Sigurd hides in a pit across Fáfnir's path to water and stabs upward. In the saga, Odin, disguised as an old man, tells him to dig extra trenches for the blood. Roasting the heart, Sigurd burns his finger and sucks it. With the dragon's blood on his tongue he understands the birds, who warn him that Regin will betray him, so he kills Regin and loads the hoard onto his horse Grani. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fáfnir
- **[MED] Ramsund carving (Sö 101).** It is cut into a flat rock near Eskilstuna, Sweden, around 1030. The dragon's body is the band that carries the runic text, framing the whole story. Similar Sigurd carvings survive at Ripon, York and Halton in England and on the Isle of Man (about 950 to 1000). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigurd_stones
- **[MED] Hylestad stave church (Setesdal, Norway).** The church was built in the late 12th or early 13th century and demolished in the 17th. Its door portal, from the second half of the 12th century, tells the legend in seven carved scenes, among them the forging of the sword Gram, the killing of Fáfnir and Sigurd sucking his thumb. It is now in the Museum of Cultural History, Oslo. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hylestad_stave_church
- **[MED] Níðhöggr.** This dragon gnaws a root of the world tree Yggdrasil and chews the corpses of murderers, adulterers and oath-breakers. The closing stanza of *Völuspá*, in Bellows's translation (1923, PD), reads: "From below the dragon dark comes forth, / Nithhogg flying from Nithafjoll" **(wording confirmed by search only)**. The original has him carrying corpses in his feathers. **[DISPUTED]** The winged image may be a late addition under Christian influence, since winged dragons are rare in Germanic art before the 13th century. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nidhogg · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_dragon
- **[MED] Dragon ships.** In Old Norse, *dreki* (dragon) was a name for the largest warships, of 30 rowing "rooms" or more, which carried carved dragon heads and tails. Olaf Tryggvason's *Ormrinn langi* (Long Serpent), with 34 rooms, was built over the winter of 999 to 1000. **[DISPUTED]** Judith Jesch says *dreki* "was not a technical term" and may be a poet's conceit. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longship

## 6. The Wawel Dragon of Kraków

- **[MED] The earliest version.** Wincenty Kadłubek's *Chronica Polonorum* was written between 1190 and 1208. In King Krak's reign, a beast called *olophagus* ("one who swallows whole") lives in a rock and demands cattle every week, or else people. Krak's sons feed it a cattle skin stuffed with smouldering sulphur. The younger then murders his brother to take the glory, is exposed and banished, and Princess Wanda inherits. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawel_Dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon
- **[MED] Długosz, 15th century.** Długosz (1415 to 1480) makes the plan the king's own: carcasses packed with sulphur, tinder, wax, pitch and tar, set alight. The dragon dies "breathing fire", and he puts its cave under Wawel Hill. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawel_Dragon
- **[LATER] The cobbler.** He first appears in Marcin Bielski's *Kronika Polska* (1597). The cobbler Skub's bait makes the dragon drink water until it dies, and Bielski points to its cave, Smocza Jama. The dragon bursting after drinking the Vistula is a later popular version. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawel_Dragon
- **[MED] Sources and images.** The 14th-century *Dzierzwa Chronicle* says Krak's sons killed the dragon "like Daniel killed the dragon of Babylon", a pointer to the biblical model. Sebastian Münster's *Cosmographia* (1544) printed a woodcut of the dragon. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawel_Dragon
- **[DISPUTED] The bones.** Bones hang on a chain outside Wawel Cathedral. Wikipedia describes them as "bones of whales or Pleistocene creatures … carried to the cathedral in medieval times as the remains of a dragon". Guides name whale, mammoth and rhinoceros, but no published study was found, and written mentions go back to the 16th century. **(search only:** https://www.jasoncolavito.com/blog/the-fossil-dragon-bones-of-polands-wawel-cathedral**)** The saying that the world ends if they fall is marked "citation needed". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawel_Dragon

## 7. The red and white dragons and the Welsh flag

- **[MED] The first telling.** The *Historia Brittonum* dates from about 829 to 830, in "the fourth year of king Mermenus" (Merfyn Frych of Gwynedd). Its traditional author, "Nennius", is doubted: Dumville argued that the preface naming him is a late forgery. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Brittonum
- **[MED] The story (chapters 40–42).** Vortigern's fortress at Dinas Emrys in Snowdonia keeps collapsing. His wise men demand the blood of a boy with no father. The boy, Ambrosius (Emrys), reveals a hidden pool with two *vermes*, one red and one white, which fight when uncovered. The red is the Britons and will in the end defeat the white, the Saxons. This is the first clearly red Welsh dragon. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dragon
- **[MED/LATER] Geoffrey of Monmouth.** His *Historia Regum Britanniae* (about 1136) turns the boy into Merlin. It also has Uther see a dragon-shaped comet, take the name "Pendragon" and make two golden dragons, one of them his standard. In Welsh "Pendragon" meant "chief warrior", and Geoffrey misread it as "dragon's head". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uther_Pendragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Regum_Britanniae
- **[MED] Older dragon imagery.** Around 540 Gildas called King Maelgwn the "insularis draco" (dragon of the island), and Welsh poets used *dragon* to mean a war leader. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Dragon
- **[DISPUTED] The archaeology.** Excavations at Dinas Emrys (1910 and 1954–56) found a pool with a platform above it, but the platform dates from long after Vortigern. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinas_Emrys
- **[MED] From banner to flag.** Owain Glyndŵr raised a golden dragon in 1400–01. Henry VII carried a red dragon standard at Bosworth (1485) and then had it carried in state to St Paul's. The green and white field is the Tudor livery. The flag became official only in 1959. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Wales

## 8. Dragons as standards and in heraldry

- **[MED] The Roman *draco*.** It was a cavalry standard with a metal dragon head and a cloth tube for a body, probably adopted from Dacian and Sarmatian units in the 2nd century. Arrian (modern translation) says that in a charge it was "inflated by the wind, and even make a sort of hissing sound". The only fully preserved head comes from Niederbieber and is now in Koblenz. A 9th-century Carolingian manuscript shows cavalry still riding under one. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draco_(military_standard)
- **[MED] Sutton Hoo.** The helmet, buried around 620 to 625, has three dragon heads, and its face mask reads as "a soaring dragon with outstretched wings". The shield carried a flying dragon. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_Hoo_helmet · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton_Hoo
- **[MED] Burford and Hastings.** Henry of Huntingdon, writing in the 12th century, says the West Saxons raised a golden dragon at Burford in 752, which is a late source. The Bayeux Tapestry shows a fallen golden dragon and a dragon standard at Harold's death, 14 October 1066. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wessex · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayeux_Tapestry
- **[LATER] The Wessex wyvern.** "There is no evidence that it explicitly identified Wessex." It was popularised in the 19th century, and the flag is from 1974. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wessex
- **[DISPUTED] Richard I and Henry III.** Secondary sources say Henry III marched out at Lewes (14 May 1264) behind a jewelled dragon standard, and that the *Itinerarium Regis Ricardi* (1220s) has Richard I hand "his dragon" to a standard-bearer in 1191. Neither was checked against a primary text. **(search only:** https://medieval.substack.com/p/the-standard-of-richard-the-lionheart**)**
- **[LATER] Dragon or wyvern.** "Wyver" first appears in a heraldic roll in 1312. The rule that a dragon has four legs and a wyvern two was fixed in English heraldry in the 16th century (Gerard Legh, 1562), and other countries never made it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyvern

## 9. Physical "evidence"

- **[MED] The Klagenfurt skull.** Probably around 1335, a woolly rhinoceros skull was dug out of a gravel pit outside Klagenfurt and displayed in the town hall as a dragon's skull. Franz Unger identified it in 1840, and it has been in the State Museum for Carinthia since 1849. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindwurmbrunnen
- **The Klagenfurt statue.** Carving began at the quarry in 1590. In 1593 the six-tonne dragon was hauled into town on rollers by "300 festively dressed boys", and it went onto its pedestal beside Hercules in 1636. **[DISPUTED]** Unger and later Othenio Abel thought the skull was the model, and Adrienne Mayor notes the statue is "often cited as the earliest reconstruction of an extinct animal". Mark Witton is cautious, and other scholars call the link "forced euhemerism". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindwurmbrunnen
- **[LATER] The Brno "dragon".** A stuffed female Nile crocodile, about 5 m long, hangs in the Old Town Hall passage. Legends say a butcher killed it with a cowhide full of quicklime, or that the Ottomans gave it to Margrave Matthias. The often-quoted date of 1608 is tradition only **(search only)**. Seville Cathedral's "Door of the Lizard" is named after a stuffed crocodile hanging from its ceiling. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brno · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seville_Cathedral
- **[MED] Dragon's blood.** The red resin of *Dracaena* trees (Socotra in antiquity, the Canaries from the 15th century) was used as a dye, varnish and medicine, and in medieval magic and alchemy. "Some medieval encyclopedias claimed its source as the literal blood of dragons and elephants which had perished in mortal combat." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon%27s_blood
- **[MED] The dracontias stone.** Pliny (*Natural History* 37.57) says it forms in a dragon's brain, but unless the head is cut off while the animal lives, "it will never assume the form of a gem, through spite on the part of the serpent" (Bostock and Riley 1855, PD; **search only**). Isidore (*Etym.* XVI.14.7) repeats it. Fra Mauro's world map (around 1450) places dragons near Herat "in whose forehead is a stone that cures many infirmities". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Here_be_dragons
- **[MED, 1558] Fake dragons.** In *Historiae animalium* IV (1558), Conrad Gessner warned that "Jenny Hanivers", dried rays carved into little monsters, were not real dragons. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_Haniver

## 10. "Here be dragons"

- **The globe.** The Hunt–Lenox Globe is a hollow copper globe about 112 mm across, now in the New York Public Library. Sources date it variously to 1504, about 1508 and about 1510, so say "early 1500s". Off the east coast of Asia it reads **HC SVNT DRACONES**. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunt–Lenox_Globe
- **[MYTH] Common on old maps?** No. The globe is "the only known historical use" of the phrase, and the English "here be dragons" is modern. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Here_be_dragons
- **How it was found.** Richard Morris Hunt bought it in Paris in 1855 "for a song" and let his children play with it. Henry Stevens recognised its value in 1870 and urged Hunt to put it in the Lenox Library. It went to James Lenox, whose collection became part of the New York Public Library. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunt–Lenox_Globe
- **[MED] Dragons that are on maps.** The Psalter world map (c. 1250 to 1265) has dragons, standing for sin, below the world. The Borgia map (around 1430) has Asian "serpents so large that they could eat an ox whole", and Fra Mauro (around 1450) an "Isola de' dragoni". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Here_be_dragons
- **[DISPUTED]** Do not call "hic sunt leones" the standard phrase either. The attested example cited is "hic abundant leones" on a 10th-century map. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Here_be_dragons

## 11. Fire, venom, wings and legs

- **[MED] Biblical and classical fire.** Job 41 has Leviathan breathe fire and smoke. In Greek myth Typhon breathed fire, and Ovid gave winged dragons fiery breath. So Beowulf is the first fire-breather of *medieval* literature, not of all literature. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyvern
- **[MED] Three medieval traditions.**
  - Heroic poetry gives dragons fire. Beowulf, composed c. 700 to 1000, is the first, while Germanic worms usually spat *atter* (venom) and only later fire.
  - Saints' lives give them poison breath. In the *Golden Legend*, "there was at Rome a dragon in a pit, which every day slew with his breath more than three hundred men" (Caxton, PD).
  - Natural history (Pliny, Isidore, the bestiaries) gives them no venom at all: a constrictor with a killing tail.

  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend
- **[MED] Shape.** Old English *wyrm* and Old Norse *ormr* mean serpent, and early Germanic dragons have no legs or wings. Wings arrive mainly from the 13th century, under continental influence. Harley 3244 (around 1260) has four legs, and otherwise four-legged dragons are rare until the 15th century, for example in Lambeth Palace MS 6. Swedish lindworms stay limbless, while Klagenfurt's has four legs and wings. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_dragon · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindworm
- **[MED] Revelation.** The King James Bible (1611, PD) has "a great red dragon, having seven heads and ten horns" (Revelation 12:3). Through the Vulgate, *draco* became the standard image of Satan. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation_12

## 12. Other dragon-slaying saints

- **[MED] St Michael, Revelation 12 (King James Bible, 1611, PD).** "And there was war in heaven: Michael and his angels fought against the dragon … And the great dragon was cast out, that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan." His image as a dragon-slaying warrior was helped by a painting set up at the Michaelion after Constantine's victory of 324. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation_12 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_(archangel)
- **[MED] St Margaret of Antioch** (died c. 304). Satan, as a dragon, swallows her, and she escapes when the cross she carries irritates its insides. Jacobus de Voragine himself called this "apocryphal and not to be taken seriously" (Ryan's translation, not PD). It became a favourite Gothic image, for example at Chartres. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_the_Virgin · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend
- **[MED] St Martha and the Tarasque.** A river dragon on the Rhône at Nerluc (now Tarascon), "thicker than an ox, longer than a horse", is the offspring of Leviathan. Martha tames it with holy water and a cross, leads it on her girdle, and the townspeople stone it to death. The legend probably arose in 12th-century Provence. **[LATER]** By tradition René of Anjou began the Tarasque procession in 1474, and UNESCO listed it in 2005. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarasque
- **[MED] St Silvester.** In the *Golden Legend* he goes down 150 steps with two lanterns, ties the Roman dragon's mouth shut with thread and seals it with the cross. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend
- **[LATER] St Romanus and the Gargouille of Rouen.** Romanus lived in the 7th century, but the dragon is "recorded for the first time in 1394". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanus_of_Rouen

## Myths to avoid

1. **"Medieval maps wrote 'Here be dragons'."** There is one known example: the Latin on the Hunt–Lenox Globe, from the early 1500s. (Section 10)
2. **"Medieval dragons all breathed fire."** Bestiaries gave them a killing tail and no venom, saints' lives gave them poison breath, and fire is mainly heroic poetry. (Section 11)
3. **"St George's dragon is part of the historical saint."** It was transferred from St Theodore in the 11th century, and the Libya setting comes from the 1260s. (Section 3)
4. **"Sabra" and "Ascalon" are medieval.** They are from 1596. (Section 3)
5. **"Merlin found the dragons."** In the original (around 829) the boy is Ambrosius, and Geoffrey made him Merlin around 1136. (Section 7)
6. **"The Welsh flag is ancient."** The red dragon standard dates from 1485, and the official flag from 1959. (Section 7)
7. **"Wessex flew a wyvern."** There is no evidence. The association is 19th century and the flag dates from 1974. (Section 8)
8. **"The four-legs/two-legs rule is medieval."** It belongs to 16th-century English heraldry. (Section 8)
9. **"The 793 dragons were definitely the aurora or meteors."** That cannot be known. (Section 1)
10. **"Lindisfarne fell in January."** The January date is probably a slip, and 8 June is accepted. (Section 1)
11. **"Beowulf is 8th-century."** The date is disputed, anywhere from about 700 to the early 1000s. (Section 2)
12. **"A cobbler killed the Wawel dragon."** He first appears in 1597. The c. 1200 original has the king's sons and a murder. (Section 6)
13. **"The Wawel bones have been scientifically identified."** No published study was found. (Section 6)
14. **"The Klagenfurt statue was definitely copied from the rhino skull."** That is a disputed hypothesis from 1840. (Section 9)
15. **"Every Viking ship was a dragon-headed *drakkar*."** *Dreki* was used for the largest ships, possibly only poetically, and *drakkar* appears in no source checked. (Section 5)
16. **"Uther Pendragon means 'dragon's head'."** That is Geoffrey's misreading. (Section 7)
17. **"Medieval people believed every dragon tale."** Jacobus doubted Margaret's dragon, Gessner exposed fakes, and scholars debate how literally bestiaries were read. (Sections 4, 9, 12)
18. **Dating folk legends as medieval.** The Lambton Worm is best known from a song of 1867, and the Gargouille appears first in 1394. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambton_Worm (Section 12)

## Best hooks (ranked)

1. Only one known old map says "here be dragons": a copper globe about 11 cm across from the early 1500s, bought in Paris "for a song" in 1855 and handed to the buyer's children as a toy. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunt–Lenox_Globe
2. Around 1335, a woolly rhinoceros skull dug out of a gravel pit was displayed in Klagenfurt's town hall as a dragon's skull, and the city's 1590s dragon statue may be modelled on it, "often cited as the earliest reconstruction of an extinct animal". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindwurmbrunnen
3. In 793 the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* saw "fiery dragons flying across the firmament" over Northumbria just before the Vikings hit Lindisfarne, and its January date for the raid is probably a scribe's slip for June. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne
4. In the oldest Wawel Dragon story (c. 1200), the prince who killed the dragon with burning sulphur then murdered his brother to steal the credit; the cobbler hero appears only in 1597. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wawel_Dragon
5. Medieval natural history says a dragon's power is in its tail, not its teeth: it ambushes elephants, ties their legs with its tail and suffocates them. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_dragon
6. Beowulf, medieval Europe's first fire-breathing dragon, survives in one manuscript that was scorched in the 1731 Cotton library fire, as a scholar leapt from a window clutching another priceless codex. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowell_Codex
7. The *Golden Legend*'s Rome had a dragon in a pit that "every day slew with his breath more than three hundred men" until St Silvester went down 150 steps and tied its mouth shut with thread. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend
8. The man who made St George's dragon famous dismissed St Margaret's escape from a dragon's belly as "apocryphal and not to be taken seriously". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend
9. Brno's town-hall "dragon" is a stuffed five-metre Nile crocodile. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brno
10. On a Swedish rock carved around 1030, Sigurd burns his thumb on a dragon's roasting heart, sucks it, and suddenly understands the birds. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigurd_stones

**Also strong:**
- The Sutton Hoo helmet's face is a dragon in flight.
- In 1558 Gessner warned buyers that "baby dragons" were carved rays.
- "Pendragon" was a mistranslation.

## Suggested narrative arc (4 to 5 minutes, about 700 words of narration)

**Cold open (0:00–0:25): "Signs in the sky" (topic 1)**
Narration: Northumbria, 793, and the *Chronicle*'s fiery dragons. Then a hard cut to the raid.
Visuals:
- Night over Lindisfarne priory, with fire streaking the sky that could be meteors or dragons.
- A manuscript line, *fyrenne dracan*, glowing.
- Dragon-prowed longships at dawn.

**1. What a dragon was (0:25–1:05) (topics 4, 11, 8)**
Narration: *draco* meant a huge serpent. In Isidore and the bestiaries it is crested, kills with its tail, hunts elephants and fears the panther's breath. Most of these sources give it no fire.
Visuals:
- A bestiary page that opens into a 3D serpent knotting an elephant's legs.
- A panther exhaling a golden haze while a dragon slides into a cave.
- A Roman cavalry *draco* inflating and hissing in the wind.

**2. The hoard (1:05–1:50) (topics 2, 5)**
Narration: Beowulf's stolen cup, the fire, Wiglaf and the king's death. The one manuscript and the 1731 fire. Fáfnir, Sigurd in the pit, and the blood that lets him understand birds.
Visuals:
- A burial mound full of gold, a single cup lifted out and the dragon's eye opening.
- Fire rolling over thatched halls.
- Manuscript edges curling in flame.
- The Ramsund serpent band animating scene by scene.
- The Sutton Hoo helmet turning to show the dragon in its face.

**3. Saints against serpents (1:50–2:35) (topics 3, 12)**
Narration: Michael in Revelation. George at Silene, with the lance, the girdle-leash and the 1260s *Golden Legend*. Then Margaret, Martha and Silvester, and the twist that Jacobus himself doubted Margaret's dragon.
Visuals:
- St George's lance striking in slow motion beside a lake.
- The princess's girdle tightening round the dragon's neck.
- Four ox-carts hauling the carcass.
- Silvester descending 150 steps with two lanterns.
- The Tarasque, turtle-shelled, on the Rhône.

**4. Dragons of nations (2:35–3:20) (topics 7, 8, 6)**
Narration: Vortigern's collapsing tower and the red and white dragons in the pool. The Bayeux dragon at Harold's death, Henry VII's red dragon at Bosworth, and the flag made official in 1959. Then Kraków: the sulphur bait and the brother's murder.
Visuals:
- A tower collapsing on Dinas Emrys and the ground opening on two coiled dragons.
- An embroidered dragon standard falling.
- A red banner over Bosworth.
- A burning, sulphur-stuffed hide rolled toward a cave under Wawel Hill.

**5. The evidence (3:20–4:05) (topic 9)**
Narration: Klagenfurt's rhino skull, Wawel's bones, Brno's crocodile, dragon's blood, the dracontias stone, and Gessner's 1558 warning about fakes.
Visuals:
- A woolly-rhino skull on a town-hall table, morphing into the 1590s stone dragon.
- Bones swinging on a chain at a cathedral door.
- A crocodile hanging from a vaulted ceiling.
- Red resin beading on a dragon tree.

**6. The edge of the map (4:05–4:35) (topic 10)**
Narration: The only "here be dragons" in the record is one engraved line on one small globe.
Visuals:
- A copper globe rotating, with the camera pushing in to **HC SVNT DRACONES** off Asia.
- The Psalter map's dragons coiled beneath the world.

**Ending (4:35–4:55)**
Narration: Medieval Europe used "dragon" for what it could not explain: a light in the sky, a skull in the gravel, a crocodile from far away, the Devil himself.
Visuals:
- A meteor over the sea at Lindisfarne fading into an illuminated initial.
