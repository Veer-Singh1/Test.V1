# Fact check

Every claim and number in the narration or on screen, with its source.

**How the sources were checked.** The build sandbox could not open nasa.gov, esa.int or
ntrs.nasa.gov directly (the network policy blocked them, and so did the fetch tool). Each source
below was checked through web-search results, which quote the page text, on 16 Nov 2026.
Figures that are approximate are shown on screen with "~", "≈", "about" or "roughly".

| # | Claim (as used) | Where used | Source(s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A rocket moves by throwing mass (exhaust) out the back; the push on the exhaust gives an equal and opposite push on the rocket (Newton's third law). | 5–11 s narration; "exhaust pushed DOWN" / "rocket pushed UP" arrows | NASA Glenn, *Beginner's Guide to Aeronautics: Rocket Thrust*: https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/rocket-thrust/ · NASA *Rockets Educator Guide*, "Newton's Laws of Motion": https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/rockets-guide-20-newtons-laws.pdf | Glenn: thrust "is produced according to Newton's third law of motion". |
| 2 | No air is needed to push against; rockets work in a vacuum. | "No air needed: it even works in empty space."; "NO AIR NEEDED" | NASA Glenn, *Rocket Thrust* (above): a rocket works in a vacuum, unlike a turbine or propeller · NASA Glenn, *Liquid Rocket Engine*: https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/lrockth.html (the oxidiser is carried on board, so no outside air is used) | |
| 3 | Roughly 90% of a rocket's mass on the launch pad is propellant (fuel + oxidiser). | 14–24 s narration; "~90%", "PROPELLANT", "everything else ~10%" bar | NASA (Bramlet, *Saturn V Launch Vehicle Program*, 1972): launch weight about 6 million lb, of which 5.56 million lb was propellant: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19720063751/downloads/19720063751.pdf · NASA Saturn V press kit (1967): 6,220,025 lb fully fueled: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19670031260/downloads/19670031260.pdf · LibreTexts (university physics text), *Mass Fraction*, which uses 85% propellant for a launch to low Earth orbit: https://phys.libretexts.org/Courses/Prince_Georges_Community_College/General_Physics_I%3A_Classical_Mechanics/33%3A_Rockets/33.02%3A_Mass_Fraction | 5.56 / 6.22 ≈ 89%; 5.56 / 6.0 ≈ 93% (my arithmetic). | 4 | Rockets are built in stages; when a stage is empty it is dropped, so the rest of the rocket isn't carrying dead weight. | 24–34 s; "DROP THE DEAD WEIGHT", "empty tank: dropped" | NASA *Rockets Educator Guide* (staging: when the first stage is empty it drops off, then the second stage fires and is dropped in turn): https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/rockets-educator-guide-20.pdf · NASA History Office, *Rocket Vehicles*: https://www.hq.nasa.gov/office/pao/History/conghand/vehicles.htm · NASA *Rocket Staging* lesson: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2010/11/492443main_ln_rocket_staging_ed.pdf | |
| 5 | A lighter rocket gets more speed from the same fuel. | "Lighter rocket, more speed from the same fuel."; "lighter = more speed" | Same as #4 (the dropped mass no longer has to be accelerated) · NASA Glenn, *Rocket Thrust* (above): the same thrust on less mass gives more acceleration (Newton's second law) | Stated qualitatively; no number. |
| 6 | Newton imagined a cannon on a very high mountain: faster shots land farther away, and fast enough, the ball falls all the way round Earth (orbit = falling around the planet). | 34–48 s; "NEWTON'S CANNON", "IT NEVER LANDS", "ORBIT = FALLING SIDEWAYS"; credit "(1728)" | NASA, *Basics of Space Flight*, ch. 3: "…accelerate it until it is going so fast that as it falls, it falls completely around the planet": https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter3-4/ · Newton, *A Treatise of the System of the World* (1728), via Wikipedia, *Newton's cannonball*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_cannonball | The trajectories in the animation are simulated with inverse-square gravity (scenes/05_orbit_cannon.tsx); the mountain height is exaggerated, as in Newton's own diagram. |
| 7 | Low Earth orbit needs about 28,000 km/h (≈ 7.8 km per second). | 48–51 s; "28,000 KM/H", "≈ 7.8 km every second", "orbit ~28,000 km/h" | NASA, *What Is the International Space Station?*: travels at about 17,500 mph (28,000 km/h) and orbits Earth every 90 minutes: https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-the-international-space-station-grades-5-8/ · ESA, *Low Earth orbit*: satellites in LEO travel at about 7.8 km/s and orbit in about 90 minutes: https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2020/03/Low_Earth_orbit · ESA, *Types of orbits*: https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Transportation/Types_of_orbits | The required speed falls with altitude (ESA: about 7.3 km/s near 1,000 km), so "about" is used. 7.8 km/s × 3,600 = 28,080 km/h. |

| 9 | Orbit is about thirty times faster than an airliner. | "That's about thirty times faster than an airliner."; "≈ 30× FASTER" | Derived from #7 and #8: 28,000 / 900 ≈ 31 (28,000 / 930 ≈ 30; 28,000 / 830 ≈ 34) | Rounded down to "about thirty". |
| 10 | Hook framing: to reach orbit you go up, then mostly sideways, very fast. | 0–5 s; "UP?" crossed out, "SIDEWAYS!" | NASA *Basics of Space Flight*, ch. 3 (as #6): "raise it up and accelerate it…" · #7 for "very fast" | |

**Cut or softened**
- No specific rocket (Saturn V or otherwise) is named on screen, because propellant fractions
  vary between vehicles. The on-screen figure is "~90%", typical rather than exact.
- The low Earth orbit altitude (about 160–2,000 km, per ESA) is not shown, to keep the number beat simple.
