## Scene 1 — Hook · ~0:30

**Narration:**
> Look closely at a rock and you're looking at a story millions of years long. Some rocks were born in fire, from molten rock deep underground. Some were built grain by grain at the bottom of ancient seas. And some were squeezed and baked until they became something completely new. Today we'll read those stories: igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rock — how each one forms, how to recognise it, and how one turns into another.

## Scene 3 — The lithosphere and Earth's layers · ~0:36

**Narration:**
> Rocks belong to the lithosphere: the crust plus the rigid top of the mantle. When the young Earth was extremely hot, the heaviest materials sank to the centre and the lightest floated to the top, so the planet formed layers. The crust we live on is thin — about five to ten kilometres under the oceans, and up to about fifty under the continents. Below it lies the mantle, about two thousand nine hundred kilometres thick, then the liquid outer core and the solid inner core, both mostly iron and nickel.

**Visuals:** Manim cut-away Earth: dense dots sink while light dots rise. The layers settle, then each is labelled as it's named. The lithosphere band highlights.
**On-screen text:** "Lithosphere = crust + rigid upper mantle" · "Crust: 5–10 km (oceans), up to ~50 km (continents)" · "Mantle: ~2 900 km" · "Outer core: liquid iron & nickel" · "Inner core: solid iron & nickel"

## Scene 4 — Minerals and rocks · ~0:30

**Narration:**
> Now, two words that are easy to mix up. A mineral is a naturally occurring, non-living solid with a definite chemical composition. Quartz, mica, feldspar and gold are all minerals. A rock is a solid mixture of one or more minerals. Think of the minerals as the ingredients, and the rock as the baked cake. Geologists sort rocks into three types, and they group them by one thing: how the rock formed.

**Visuals:** four mineral icons (quartz crystal, mica sheets, feldspar block, gold nugget) slide together into a speckled granite swatch. Three rock-type cards appear.
**On-screen text:** "Mineral: natural, non-living solid, definite chemical composition" · "Rock: a mixture of one or more minerals" · "Grouped by HOW they formed"

## Scene 5 — Igneous rock: how it forms · ~0:38

**Narration:**
> Igneous means 'fiery'. Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and hardens, and they were the first rocks to form in the Earth's crust. It starts when a crack or weakness opens in the crust. The pressure drops, and magma — molten rock below the surface — pushes upwards along the line of weakness. If the magma is forced in among other rocks and cools inside the crust, it forms intrusive rock: intrusive means 'forced in'. If it pours out onto the surface as lava and cools there, it forms extrusive rock: 'forced out'.

**Visuals:** Manim cross-section of the crust. A crack opens, magma rises from a chamber, part of it cools inside the crust (labelled intrusive) and part erupts from a volcano as lava (labelled extrusive).
**On-screen text:** "Igneous = fiery" · "Magma: molten rock below the surface" · "Lava: molten rock on the surface" · "Intrusive: cools inside the crust" · "Extrusive: cools on the surface"

## Scene 6 — Igneous rock: characteristics · ~0:50

**Narration:**
> So how do you recognise igneous rock? First, it's crystalline: as magma cools, silica, iron and magnesium combine into mineral crystals. The speed of cooling decides their size. Deep underground, magma cools slowly, so the crystals have time to grow large — that's granite. At the surface, lava cools quickly and the crystals stay tiny — that's basalt. Igneous rock is also massive, meaning it has no layers, and it contains no fossils, because the heat destroys any living material. Granite, gabbro and dolerite are intrusive. Basalt, pumice, obsidian and scoria are extrusive — and pumice is so full of gas bubbles that it can float on water.

**Visuals:** two rock swatches side by side. Crystals grow slowly into large grains (granite) and quickly into fine grains (basalt). A checklist builds, then an examples table.
**On-screen text:** "Slow cooling → large crystals (granite)" · "Fast cooling → tiny crystals (basalt)" · "Crystalline · No layers · No fossils" · "Intrusive: granite, gabbro, dolerite" · "Extrusive: basalt, pumice, obsidian, scoria"

## Scene 7 — Sedimentary rock: four steps · ~0:41

**Narration:**
> Sedimentary rocks are built from sediment, and the most common way is mechanical, in four steps. One: erosion. Rivers, wind and ice wear rock down into smaller pieces, bits of clay and dissolved substances. Two: transport and deposition. The pieces are carried away and dropped at the bottom of a sea, lake or valley. Three: new layers settle on top of older ones, over thousands of years. Four: compaction and cementation. The weight of the water and the layers above squashes the lower layers, and dissolved minerals cement the particles together.

**Visuals:** Manim landscape: a river carries particles from a hill into the sea. Grains settle into a basin, layers stack up, arrows press down and the grains lock together. A numbered step list builds beside it.
**On-screen text:** "1 Erosion" · "2 Transport & deposition" · "3 Layers build up (thousands of years)" · "4 Compaction & cementation"

## Scene 8 — Sedimentary rock: other ways and characteristics · ~0:43

**Narration:**
> Sedimentary rock can also form chemically, when water evaporates and leaves minerals behind, like rock salt and some limestone. Or organically, from the compressed remains of living things, like coal and shelly limestone. You can spot sedimentary rock by its visible layers, called strata. It's usually softer, and wears away more easily than igneous rock. And it's the rock type where fossils are found. Sandstone is made of sand grains, shale of clay and mud, conglomerate of rounded pebbles, limestone of shells, coral and calcium carbonate, and coal of plant remains.

**Visuals:** an evaporating pool leaves salt cubes (chemical), plants pile up into a coal seam (organic), then a layered rock with a fossil shell. A table of five rocks and what they're made of.
**On-screen text:** "Chemical: rock salt, some limestone" · "Organic: coal, shelly limestone" · "Strata · Softer · Fossils" · rock table

## Scene 9 — Metamorphic rock: heat and pressure · ~0:44

**Narration:**
> Metamorphic means 'changed in form'. These rocks start out as igneous, sedimentary or even other metamorphic rock, and they're changed by heat, pressure, or both — without melting completely. The heat comes from magma pushing into the crust, sometimes as a huge intrusion called a batholith. It makes the existing crystals re-form as new crystals. The pressure comes from the weight of the rock above, or from tectonic plates colliding and folding the crust, and it often squeezes the new crystals into bands. Metamorphic rocks are usually harder and denser than the rock they came from, and they contain no fossils.

**Visuals:** a rock block sits above a glowing batholith and its crystals re-form. Arrows squeeze from both sides and the grains line up into bands. A checklist follows.
**On-screen text:** "Metamorphic = changed in form" · "Heat · Pressure · No complete melting" · "Batholith: huge magma intrusion" · "Harder · Denser · Often banded · No fossils"

## Scene 10 — The big five changes · ~0:22

**Narration:**
> Learn these five changes. Shale becomes slate. Limestone becomes marble. Sandstone becomes quartzite. Granite becomes gneiss. And coal becomes graphite. Two memory tricks: shale and slate start with the same letter, and sand is mostly quartz, so sandstone becomes quartzite.

**Visuals:** five rows build one by one, each an original rock swatch → arrow → metamorphic swatch.
**On-screen text:** "Shale → Slate · Limestone → Marble · Sandstone → Quartzite · Granite → Gneiss · Coal → Graphite"

## Scene 11 — The rock cycle · ~0:46

**Narration:**
> None of these rocks lasts forever. Over hundreds of millions of years, rocks are melted, worn away, buried and changed, then form new rocks again. This never-ending series of changes is the rock cycle, and it has no beginning and no end. Let's follow one journey. Granite at the surface is broken down where it lies — that's weathering — and the pieces are carried off by erosion. The sand is deposited, then compacted and cemented into sandstone. Buried deep, heat and pressure turn the sandstone into quartzite. Pushed deeper still, it melts into magma. And when that magma cools slowly underground, it forms new granite.

**Visuals:** a cycle diagram (magma, igneous, sediment, sedimentary, metamorphic) with labelled arrows. A glowing token travels granite → sand → sandstone → quartzite → magma → granite, and each process lights up as it's named.
**On-screen text:** "Rock cycle: no beginning, no end" · process labels on the arrows

## Scene 12 — Three exam traps · ~0:29

**Narration:**
> Three traps that cost marks. One: weathering and erosion are not the same thing. Weathering breaks rock down where it lies; erosion wears it away and carries it off. Two: metamorphic rock doesn't melt. It changes while it's still solid — if it melts, it becomes magma. Three: the rock cycle doesn't start with igneous rock. Any rock can become any other type, so there's no start and no end.

**Visuals:** three cards, each stamped TRAP and then flipped to the FACT.
**On-screen text:** "Weathering: breaks down in place · Erosion: carries away" · "Metamorphic: changes while solid" · "No start, no end"

## Scene 13 — Recap and quiz · ~0:55

**Narration (recap):**
> Quick recap. Igneous rock forms when magma or lava cools: crystals, no layers, no fossils. Sedimentary rock forms when sediment is compacted and cemented: strata, and fossils. Metamorphic rock is changed by heat and pressure: harder, denser, often banded.
