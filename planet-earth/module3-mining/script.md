## Scene 3 — Ores and refining · ~0:29

**Narration:**
> Most metals aren't found pure. In rock, they're chemically joined to other elements, usually oxygen or sulphur. A rock that contains enough of a useful mineral to be worth the cost of mining and processing is called an ore. The key words are 'worth the cost': if there's too little of the mineral, it isn't an ore. Refining is the set of processes that separate the pure metal from everything else in the ore.

**Visuals:** a rock grain zooms into metal atoms bonded to oxygen and sulphur. A balance weighs "value of the metal" against "cost of mining and processing" and tips to "ORE".
**On-screen text:** "Metals are joined to oxygen or sulphur" · "Ore: worth the cost of mining and processing" · "Refining: separating the pure metal"

## Scene 4 — South Africa's mineral wealth · ~0:40

**Narration:**
> South Africa is one of the richest mining countries in the world. Gold comes from the Witwatersrand basin, across Gauteng, the Free State and North West. Platinum and chrome come from the Bushveld Igneous Complex in Limpopo and North West. Iron ore is mined at Sishen, and manganese in the Kalahari field, both in the Northern Cape. Coal comes mainly from Mpumalanga and Limpopo, diamonds from Kimberley and coastal deposits, and copper from Phalaborwa. Mining brings jobs, foreign income and raw materials for industry.

**Visuals:** an outline map of South Africa with a labelled marker appearing for each mineral as it's named.
**On-screen text:** marker labels: "Gold: Witwatersrand" · "Platinum & chrome: Bushveld Igneous Complex" · "Iron ore: Sishen" · "Manganese: Kalahari field" · "Coal: Mpumalanga & Limpopo" · "Diamonds: Kimberley & coast" · "Copper: Phalaborwa"

## Scene 5 — What the minerals are used for · ~0:25

**Narration:**
> These minerals end up all around you: gold in jewellery, electronics and medicine; platinum in catalytic converters, which clean car exhaust; chrome in stainless steel, and manganese to make steel stronger; iron in the steel itself; coal in power stations; diamonds in cutting and drilling tools; and copper in electrical wiring.

**Visuals:** a grid of mineral cards, each with a simple icon and its uses.
**On-screen text:** mineral → uses, for each card

## Scene 6 — Three ways to mine · ~0:51

**Narration:**
> How a mineral is mined depends on how deep the ore lies. For deep ore, like gold and platinum, we use shaft mining: a vertical shaft is sunk into the ground, and horizontal tunnels are dug out to the ore. Miners, machines and ore travel up and down in cages and lifts. For ore close to the surface, like iron, coal, copper and diamonds, we use open cast mining. The soil and rock on top, called the overburden, is stripped away, and the ore is dug from a huge pit that deepens in steps called benches. And for small deposits of gold and diamonds in riverbeds, there's panning: gravel is swirled with water in a pan, heavy particles sink, and the lighter sand washes over the edge.

**Visuals:** Manim cross-section: a shaft and headgear, a cage moving down, tunnels to the ore body → open-pit aerial (video) → Manim cross-section of the overburden being stripped and benches stepping down → a swirling pan where heavy grains sink.
**On-screen text:** "Shaft mining: deep ore (gold, platinum)" · "Open cast: ore near the surface (iron, coal, copper, diamonds)" · "Overburden" · "Benches" · "Panning: riverbed gold & diamonds"

## Scene 7 — Refining, stage 1: physical separation · ~0:27

**Narration:**
> Once the ore is out, refining begins. Stage one is physical separation, which doesn't change the substances chemically. The rock is crushed and ground into small pieces or a fine powder. Useless rock is sorted out by hand or sifted away. Then washing, sieving and gravity — panning or sluicing — separate heavy mineral particles from lighter waste.

**Visuals:** a three-step flow: a crusher squashes rock into powder, a sieve lets fines through, and water washes light grains away while heavy ones stay.
**On-screen text:** "Physical: no chemical change" · "Crush & grind" · "Sort & sift" · "Wash, sieve, pan, sluice"

## Scene 8 — Refining, stage 2: reduction · ~0:44

**Narration:**
> Stage two is chemical. Many metals occur as metal oxides, so we need to remove the oxygen. Removing oxygen from a compound is called reduction; gaining oxygen is oxidation. The most common reducing agent is carbon, as coke or charcoal. At high temperatures, carbon attracts the oxygen more strongly than the metal does, so it takes the oxygen and leaves pure molten metal behind. Take lead: lead oxide plus carbon gives lead plus carbon monoxide. The lead oxide loses oxygen, so it's reduced. The carbon gains oxygen, so it's oxidised.

**Visuals:** particle animation: a metal–oxygen pair meets a carbon atom, the oxygen jumps to the carbon, and the metal is left behind. The equation builds, with "reduced" and "oxidised" labels.
**On-screen text:** "Reduction: loses oxygen · Oxidation: gains oxygen" · "metal oxide + carbon → metal + carbon dioxide / carbon monoxide" · "PbO + C → Pb + CO"

## Scene 9 — Refining iron and lead · ~0:37

**Narration:**
> Iron is made in a blast furnace. Iron ore, coke and limestone go in at the top, and hot air is blown in at the bottom. The carbon burns to form carbon monoxide, which reduces the iron oxide to molten iron. The limestone removes sandy impurities as slag, which floats on the molten iron and is drained off. For lead, the ore galena — lead sulphide — is crushed and roasted in air, forming lead oxide and sulphur dioxide gas. The lead oxide is then reduced with coke.

**Visuals:** a blast furnace diagram: inputs at the top, hot-air blast at the bottom, a molten iron layer with slag floating on it, and two tap holes. Then a short lead flow: galena → roast → PbO + SO₂ → reduce → Pb.
**On-screen text:** "Iron ore + coke + limestone" · "CO reduces iron oxide" · "Slag floats on molten iron" · "Galena (PbS) → roast → PbO + SO₂ → + C → Pb"

## Scene 10 — Refining copper and gold · ~0:39

**Narration:**
> Copper ore is crushed and heated with oxygen, giving copper sulphide and iron oxide. Silica is added, which combines with the iron oxide to form slag, and that's removed. Heating the copper sulphide in air gives impure copper, which is then purified. Gold is different. The ore is crushed, washed and filtered, then a cyanide solution dissolves the gold, and zinc brings it back out of the solution. With only about five grams per tonne, producing one kilogram of gold means processing two hundred tonnes of ore.

**Visuals:** a copper flow chart with a slag step. A gold flow chart with cyanide and zinc steps. A worked calculation builds line by line.
**On-screen text:** "Silica removes iron oxide as slag" · "Cyanide dissolves gold · Zinc brings it out" · "1 kg = 1 000 g · 1 000 g ÷ 5 g per tonne = 200 tonnes"

## Scene 11 — Impact on the environment · ~0:46

**Narration:**
> Mining comes at a cost, and because the Earth's spheres are linked, the damage spreads. Mines use huge amounts of water and electricity. Chemicals and acid drainage from old mines leak into rivers and groundwater — the hydrosphere. Pits, waste rock and mine dumps change the land permanently — the lithosphere. Dust and gases from blasting, crushing and smelting pollute the atmosphere. And habitats are destroyed, threatening biodiversity in the biosphere. Follow one chain to the end: blasting releases dust, the dust settles on leaves, it blocks sunlight, photosynthesis drops, and a nearby farmer's crop yield falls.

**Visuals:** four sphere cards around a mine icon, each getting its impact as it's named. A cause-and-effect chain of five linked boxes builds.
**On-screen text:** sphere impacts · "Dust → settles on leaves → blocks sunlight → less photosynthesis → lower crop yield"

## Scene 12 — Impact on people, and rehabilitation · ~0:40

**Narration:**
> For people, mining is a mixed blessing. It creates jobs, builds roads and towns, and earns billions of rands in foreign income. But mine work is dangerous, and breathing in fine dust causes lung diseases such as silicosis and pneumoconiosis. That's why, by law, mining companies must rehabilitate the land when mining stops: make the site safe and stable, remove pollutants and treat the water, reshape the land and replace the topsoil stored at the start, replant indigenous plants, and restore rivers and wetlands.

**Visuals:** a two-column card (benefits in green, harms in coral), then a five-step rehabilitation checklist beside a barren landscape that turns green.
**On-screen text:** "Jobs · Infrastructure · Foreign income" · "Dangerous work · Silicosis · Pneumoconiosis" · five rehabilitation steps

**Narration (recap):**
> Quick recap. An ore is worth the cost of mining. Shaft mining reaches deep ore; open cast mining takes ore near the surface. Refining separates the metal physically, then chemically by reduction. And mines must be rehabilitated when they close.
